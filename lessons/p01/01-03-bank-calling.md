:::story The helpful security team
Elisabeth is 69 and lives in Bern. Her phone rings. A polite woman speaks perfect Swiss German. “This is the security department of your bank. Someone just tried to pay with your card in another country.”

Elisabeth is worried. The woman says she can stop the payment. First, Elisabeth just needs to read out the code that is about to arrive by text message. Then she should install a small “security app”.

A code arrives. Elisabeth is about to read it. Then she thinks: why does my own bank need a code from me? She says: “I will call the bank myself.” She hangs up and calls the number on the back of her card. Her card was never used abroad.
:::end

:::remember
**Your bank will never mind if you hang up. Call back on the number printed on your card.**
:::end

## How the trick works

The caller pretends to be your bank, its security team or its fraud department. They tell you about a danger: a strange payment, a hacked account, or “your money is not safe”. Then they offer to help, and the help is the trap.

:::figure bank-call
What a fake bank call asks for, and why each request is a warning sign.
:::end

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security warns that these callers often speak perfect Swiss German and can fake or hide their phone number. Its advice: end the call immediately ([BACS: Phishing, vishing and smishing](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)). The St. Gallen police describe the same trick as a fake “security service” of a bank ([Kantonspolizei St.Gallen](https://www.sg.ch/sicherheit/kantonspolizei/praevention--jugend--cybercrime/cybercrime/telefonbetrug.html)).

## Three requests that always mean “scam”

- **“Read me the code.”** A code sent to your phone is a key to your account. TWINT, for example, says that neither TWINT, its partners nor banks will ever ask for your security code ([TWINT: Sicher twinten](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)).
- **“Install this app so I can help.”** Remote-control apps let a stranger use your phone or computer. If you installed one because of a call, the Swiss cyber office advises you to uninstall it ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)).
- **“Move your money to a safe account.”** There is no such thing. Your bank does not need you to move money to protect it.

## What to do

1. **Stop.** Say: “I will call the bank myself.”
2. **Hang up.** Even if the caller sounds friendly or official.
3. **Check.** Call the number on the back of your bank card, or on a bank letter. The US Federal Communications Commission gives the same advice: hang up and call the number on your statement or the company’s website ([FCC](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/spoofing)).

If you already shared a code, installed an app or made a payment, call your bank at once so it can block your card or account. Many banks have a 24-hour blocking hotline ([BACS: Financial loss](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/finanzieller-schaden)). Then report it to the police. It is not your fault.

:::helper
Find the 24-hour blocking number for each bank and card your relative has, and add it to their trusted-numbers card. Explain the three requests in this lesson in your own words. Then agree: “If anyone asks for a code, an app or a money transfer, you hang up and call me or the bank.”
:::end

:::quiz
- q: A caller from “your bank” asks you to read out a code you just received. What do you do?
  options: ["Read it out quickly so they can help", "Hang up and call the number on your card", "Read only half of it"]
  answer: 1
  explain: A code is a key to your account. A real bank will not ask you to read it out.
- q: The caller asks you to install an app so they can fix the problem. Is that safe?
  options: ["Yes, if they are from the bank", "No, it would let a stranger control your phone", "Only on a computer"]
  answer: 1
  explain: Remote-control apps let the caller act as you, including in your banking.
- q: Which number should you use to call your bank back?
  options: ["The number that called you", "The number on the back of your card or on a bank letter", "A number the caller reads out to you"]
  answer: 1
  explain: The caller’s number can be faked. The number on your card is real.
:::end
