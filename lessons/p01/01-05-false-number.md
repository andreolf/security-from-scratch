:::story “But it said 117”
Marianne is 82 and lives in Basel. Her phone rings, and the screen shows 117. “That must be real,” she thinks. The man on the phone says he is a police officer and asks about her savings.

Later, her son explains: the screen can show any number the caller chooses. Real police don’t call from 117, and they never ask about savings on the phone. Marianne laughs. “So the screen is like a name tag. Anyone can write anything on it.”
:::end

:::remember
**The number on your screen proves nothing. Trust only the number that you dial yourself.**
:::end

## What “spoofing” means

When someone calls you, your phone shows a number or a name. This is called [[term:caller-id|caller ID]]. Many people think it is checked by the phone company, like a passport. It isn’t always.

Criminals can make your phone show a different number. This is called [[term:spoofing]]. The US Federal Communications Commission explains that callers can deliberately falsify the information shown, and that scammers sometimes spoof the numbers of government agencies or use a number that looks local ([FCC: Caller ID spoofing](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/spoofing)).

## It can even look like the police

Zurich police warn that the number can be manipulated so that even the real police number appears on your screen ([cybercrimepolice.ch](https://cybercrimepolice.ch/de/faelle/falsche-polizisten-rufen-wieder-aeltere-personen-an)). The same police describe recorded scam calls that appear to come from Swiss mobile numbers, but are really made over the internet ([cybercrimepolice.ch: Bandansagen](https://cybercrimepolice.ch/de/faelle/vorsicht-bei-telefonanrufen-mit-bandansagen)).

Text messages can be faked in the same way. The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security explains that the sender of a fake text can be disguised so that the message lands in the same conversation as real messages from that company ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)). So a fake message can sit right below real ones.

## What this means for you

- A familiar number or name on the screen is **not proof**.
- Even a call that shows your bank, the police or a family member can be fake.
- If the call is about **money, codes or secrets**, hang up and call back on a number you already have.

When you dial the number yourself, the call really goes to that number. That is why “hang up and call back” works so well.

:::practice caller-id

:::helper
Try the “Be the scammer” practice together. Seeing the number change as you type often makes the idea click faster than any explanation. Then repeat the simple rule: “The screen can lie. I call back myself.”
:::end
