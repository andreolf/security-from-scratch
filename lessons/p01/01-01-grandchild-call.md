:::story Rosa’s Tuesday afternoon
It is a Tuesday afternoon. Rosa is 78. She lives in Winterthur. Her phone rings.

A man speaks. He says he is from the police. He sounds serious and kind. “Your granddaughter has caused a bad car accident,” he says. “She is here with me. She is very upset.”

In the background, Rosa hears a young woman crying. It sounds like Lea.

“To keep her out of prison tonight, we need a deposit. Right away. Please don’t tell anyone. It could make things worse for her.”

Rosa’s heart is racing. She loves Lea. She wants to help. Then she remembers one thing she learned. She says: “I’m going to hang up and call my granddaughter.”

The man gets angry. He tells her there is no time. Rosa hangs up anyway. She calls Lea’s number from her own contacts. Lea answers on the second ring. She is at work. She is fine.
:::end

:::remember
**If a call makes you panic about money, hang up. Then call your family yourself, on the number you already have.**
:::end

## Why this works on loving people

This trick does not work because people are foolish. It works because people love their family. The caller wants three things: **fear** (an accident, an arrest), **hurry** (“there is no time”) and **secrecy** (“don’t tell anyone”). Those feelings stop us from thinking calmly. That is the whole trick.

## Warning signs

- Someone says a family member is in trouble and **money is needed now**.
- You are told to **keep it secret**, even from your own family.
- You hear crying, but **you can’t talk to your relative properly**.
- You are asked for **cash, gold, jewellery or crypto**, or someone offers to **come and collect it**.

## Good to know

Real police never ask for money on the phone, and they never send someone to your door to collect cash or valuables. Zurich’s cantonal police write this clearly in their warning about shock calls ([cybercrimepolice.ch](https://cybercrimepolice.ch/de/faelle/schockanruf-durch-falsche-polizisten)).

The voice can be fake. Zurich police report that in some cases the crying voice was made with artificial intelligence ([cybercrimepolice.ch](https://cybercrimepolice.ch/de/faelle/schockanruf-durch-falsche-polizisten)). The US Federal Trade Commission explains that a scammer can copy a voice from a short audio clip, and advises: don’t trust the voice, call the person on a number you know ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/scammers-use-fake-emergencies-steal-your-money)). So don’t try to recognise the voice. Check the story.

## What to do

1. **Stop.** Say: “I will call back.” You don’t have to be polite.
2. **Hang up.** Put the phone down.
3. **Check.** Call your relative on the number saved in your phone. If you can’t reach them, call another family member.
4. **Tell the police.** In Switzerland, call 117. On a landline, hang up first and wait for the dial tone before you dial ([cybercrimepolice.ch](https://cybercrimepolice.ch/de/faelle/falsche-polizisten-rufen-wieder-aeltere-personen-an)).

## A family code word

Some families agree on a secret word or phrase. If a caller says they are your grandchild, ask for the word. A scammer won’t know it. The FBI suggests exactly this ([FBI IC3 public service announcement](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). Choose a word that is not on social media, and never say it first.

:::practice call-drill scenario=grandchild

:::helper
Agree on a [[term:code-word|family code word]] at the next family meal, and write it somewhere only the family knows. Tell your parent or grandparent clearly: “If I am ever in trouble, I will never ask you for cash on the phone, and I will never be angry if you hang up and call me back.”
:::end

If you already paid or handed something over, it is not your fault. Call your bank straight away to block payments, then report it to the police.
