:::story Two links that looked the same
Sofia is 67 and runs a small sewing club in Geneva. She gets two emails on the same day. Both say they are from TWINT, the Swiss payment app. One link starts with “go.twint.ch”. The other starts with “twint.ch.” and then continues with something long.

Sofia’s grandson once showed her a trick: look for the real name just before the first single slash. In the second link, it is not twint.ch at all. She deletes that email, and opens TWINT through the app itself, just to be sure.
:::end

:::remember
**The real website name sits just before the first single slash. Everything in front of it can be decoration.**
:::end

## How to read a web address

A web address looks complicated, but you only need one part: the **website name**, also called the [[term:domain]]. Here is how to find it.

1. Skip the beginning: “https” and the two slashes after it.
2. Find the **first single slash** “/” after that.
3. Look at the words **just before it**, usually the last two parts separated by a dot. That is the real owner.

:::figure link-anatomy
Where the real website name hides in a link.
:::end

So in **mybank.ch.login-help.net/de**, the real name is **login-help.net**. The words “mybank.ch” in front are decoration, chosen to fool you. Scammers also use look-alike spelling, such as a switched letter. The FTC advises looking out for misspellings or a switched letter in links ([FTC: QR codes](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/12/scammers-hide-harmful-links-qr-codes-steal-your-information)).

TWINT publishes the structure of its official payment links: they start with go.twint.ch, after the usual “https” beginning ([TWINT: Sicher twinten](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)). Knowing what the real address looks like helps you spot a fake.

## Check the sender too

The name shown as the sender of an email can be anything. Look at the email address behind the name. But remember: even the address can be faked ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)). Reading the sender and the link helps you spot many fakes. It can’t prove that a message is real.

## Peek without opening

On a phone, a link’s address is often hidden behind words like “Click here”. You can usually see the address before you open it.

:::try See where a link goes without opening it
--- iPhone
1. In Safari or Mail, **touch and hold** the link. Don’t just tap it.
2. A preview appears, showing the address. Read the website name.
3. To close the preview without opening the page, **tap outside** it.

Checked against: [Apple Support: Browse the web using Safari on iPhone](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/browse-the-web-iph1fbef4daa/ios).
:::end

We have not yet found an official Google help page for previewing a link on Android, so this lesson doesn’t give Android steps. Whatever phone you have, the safe way in from lesson 02.04 works every time: don’t use the link at all.

:::practice link-reader

:::helper
Practise with real links from emails your relative trusts, such as a newsletter. Point to the website name together. Then explain the limit: reading links is a good check, but the safest habit is still to open banks and shops from a bookmark or the official app.
:::end
