:::story The overdue invoice
Ursula is 75 and volunteers as treasurer for her village choir. One morning an email arrives: “Invoice 88231 overdue. Payment required today to avoid legal action.” A file is attached: “Invoice_88231.zip”.

Ursula pays invoices for the choir, so she is used to them. But she has never heard of this company, and the email is rushing her. She doesn’t open the file. She asks the choir’s president, who has never heard of the company either. Ursula deletes the email.
:::end

:::remember
**If you didn’t expect a file, an invoice or a QR code, don’t open it. Ask the sender through your own path first.**
:::end

## Why attachments can be dangerous

An [[term:attachment]] is a file sent with an email or message, such as a document, a picture or a “zip” file. Most attachments are harmless. But a fake one can try to install harmful software on your phone or computer, or lead you to a fake sign-in page.

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security’s first advice on phishing is simple: don’t click on links or attachments ([BACS: Phishing](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)). The US Federal Trade Commission lists “an invoice you don’t recognise” as a typical sign of phishing ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams)).

## QR codes are links too

A [[term:qr-code|QR code]] is a square pattern you scan with your phone’s camera. It is really just a link in a different form. The FTC advises: don’t scan a QR code in an email or text message you didn’t expect ([FTC: QR codes](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/12/scammers-hide-harmful-links-qr-codes-steal-your-information)). The Swiss cyber office warns that a QR code in a fake message can lead to a copy of a real website ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)).

## Three questions before you open a file

1. **Did I expect it?** From this person, about this subject, today?
2. **Does it rush or frighten me?** “Overdue”, “legal action”, “final notice”.
3. **Can I check it another way?** Call the sender on a number you already have, or sign in through your own app or bookmark.

If the answer to the first question is “no”, or the second is “yes”, don’t open the file. Check first.

## When it really is from someone you know

Email accounts can be taken over, and the sender of an email can easily be faked ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)). So a message can show a friend’s name and still be fake. If a friend suddenly sends a file with only a short line, such as “Look at this!”, call or text them on their usual number and ask.

:::exercise The invoice question
You receive an email with a PDF called “Reminder_your_order.pdf” from an online shop you sometimes use. You don’t remember ordering anything recently. What do you do? Write down your steps.
:::solution
Don’t open the file. Open the shop’s app or your own bookmark and look at “My orders”. If there is no open order or payment, the email was fake: delete it, or report it to reports@antiphishing.ch (in Switzerland) or reportphishing@apwg.org (in the US). If you are still unsure, call the shop on the number from its official website, which you open yourself.
:::end

:::helper
Help your relative tell the difference between photos from family (expected) and files from strangers (unexpected). Make sure their phone and computer install updates automatically. Phase 05 explains why updates matter.
:::end
