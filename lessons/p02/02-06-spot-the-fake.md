:::story The Thursday email club
Every Thursday, four friends at a community centre in Zurich bring one email they weren’t sure about. They read each one aloud and look for the signs together. Some weeks, every email is fake. Some weeks, one is real.

Their favourite rule came from the youngest member, Kurt, aged 68: “We don’t need to be sure. If in doubt, we use our own door.” Nobody has lost money since they started.
:::end

:::remember
**You don’t need to be sure whether a message is fake. If in doubt, don’t use it: go in through your own door.**
:::end

## The signs you have learned

Before you start, here are the warning signs from this phase in one place.

:::figure five-signs
Five warning signs in one picture.
:::end

1. **A problem or a prize** you didn’t expect: a blocked account, an invoice, a win.
2. **Hurry or threats**: “today”, “24 hours”, “legal action”.
3. **A request**: sign in, confirm details, pay, read out a code, open a file.
4. **A general greeting** like “Dear customer”.
5. **A link or sender address that doesn’t match** the real company.

These signs come from the US Federal Trade Commission’s list of phishing signs ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams)) and the Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security’s advice ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)).

A real message can have one of these signs too. And a clever fake can have none of them. That is why the safe habit matters more than a perfect score.

## How did it go?

If you found most of the fakes, well done. If some fooled you, that is completely normal. Fakes are made by professionals, and they keep getting better. The FBI warns that criminals use AI tools to translate their messages and limit spelling and grammar mistakes, which used to be a warning sign ([FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). The AI Safety from Scratch course explains this in its lesson on AI-written phishing.

What protects you is not a perfect eye. It is the habit: when a message asks you to do something, don’t use its link, its attachment or its phone number. Use your own door.

## TWINT and other payment apps

One of the practice emails asks for a TWINT PIN and security code. TWINT says its staff will never ask for your passwords, and that TWINT, its partners and banks will never ask for your security code ([TWINT: Sicher twinten](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)). You can forward suspicious TWINT emails to abuse@twint.ch ([TWINT](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)).

:::helper
Start your own “email club”: once a week, your relative forwards or shows you one message they weren’t sure about, and you look at it together. Celebrate good questions, not just right answers. Over time, they will need you less, and they will still know they can always ask.
:::end

  options: ["TWINT sometimes asks for it", "TWINT, its partners and banks never ask for it, so it is fake", "It is real if it uses your name"]
  answer: 1
  explain: TWINT says it never asks for your security code.
- q: Why can AI make phishing harder to spot?
  options: ["It makes the emails shorter", "It helps scammers write messages with fewer mistakes", "It makes the links real"]
  answer: 1
  explain: Spelling mistakes are a weaker sign than they used to be. Rely on your own door.
:::end
