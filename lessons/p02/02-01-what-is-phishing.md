:::story The email from “the bank”
Thomas is 70 and lives in Lucerne. An email arrives with his bank’s logo. “Dear customer, we have detected unusual activity. Please confirm your details within 24 hours, or your account will be blocked.” There is a big blue button: “Confirm now”.

Thomas notices three things. The email says “Dear customer”, not his name. It hurries him. And it wants his details through a button. He doesn’t click. He opens his banking app the usual way. There is no message, and nothing is blocked.
:::end

:::remember
**If a message wants you to click, pay or sign in quickly, don’t use its link. Go in the way you always do.**
:::end

## What “phishing” means

[[term:phishing|Phishing]] sounds like “fishing”, and that is the idea. The criminal throws out a hook, such as a fake email or text, and waits for someone to bite. The bait might be a problem with your account, a parcel, a refund or a prize. The hook is a link, a button, an attachment or a phone number.

:::figure phishing-hook
The bait, the hook and the catch: how a phishing message works.
:::end

## Five warning signs

The US Federal Trade Commission lists signs like these ([FTC: How to recognize and avoid phishing scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams)):

1. It says there is **suspicious activity** or a **problem with your account**.
2. It asks you to **confirm personal or payment details**.
3. It includes an **invoice you don’t recognise**.
4. It wants you to **click a link to make a payment**.
5. It uses a **general greeting**, like “Dear customer”.

Add one more sign: it **hurries** you. “Within 24 hours.” “Last warning.”

## What banks never do

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security says that no bank will ever ask you by email to change your password or to verify your card details ([BACS: Phishing](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)). It also reminds us that the sender of an email or text can easily be faked. So a familiar name or logo proves nothing.

## A calm question to ask

The FTC suggests asking yourself: “Do I have an account with this company?” If you don’t, it is fake. If you do, don’t use the message. Contact the company with a phone number or website you know is real ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams)).

## Reporting phishing

In Switzerland, you can forward phishing emails to reports@antiphishing.ch, which is run by the Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security ([antiphishing.ch](https://www.antiphishing.ch/)). In the US, the FTC says you can forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams)). Then delete the message.

:::exercise Find the signs
Read Thomas’s email in the story again. Write down every warning sign you can find.
:::solution
“Dear customer” (a general greeting). “Unusual activity” (a problem with your account). “Confirm your details” (asks for personal information). “Within 24 hours … will be blocked” (hurry and fear). A “Confirm now” button (a link to sign in). That is five signs in four short sentences.
:::end

:::helper
Help your relative find the “report phishing” or “spam” option in their email app, and show them that deleting is always fine. If they forward you suspicious emails to ask, thank them every time, even if the email was real. Asking is exactly the right habit.
:::end
