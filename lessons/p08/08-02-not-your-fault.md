:::story Hans tells his son
Hans is 79, a retired engineer in Aarau. For months he sent money to a woman he met online. When he finally understood, he felt foolish. “An engineer, falling for this,” he thought. He told nobody for weeks.

When his son visited, Hans said it at last. His son didn’t laugh. He sat with him, went with him to the police, and helped him call the bank. Later Hans said the worst part had been the silence, not the money.

*Hans is made up.*
:::end

:::remember
**Being scammed is not a sign of stupidity. Scammers are skilled at using feelings. Talking about it is brave, and it protects you and others.**
:::end

## How scammers really work

Scams are not clumsy tricks that only fool careless people. The FBI writes that cryptocurrency investment scams are mostly run by organised criminal groups using “psychological manipulation” and “the appearance of legitimacy” ([FBI IC3: 2025 report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)).

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) explains some of the tricks in a summer series ([BACS: Woche 31](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w31-de)):

- **Loneliness and love.** Romance scammers deliberately pick people who are lonely and give them hope of a relationship. Once there is an emotional bond, later demands are easier, and many people find it hard to get out alone ([BACS: Woche 32](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w32-de)).
- **The “foot in the door”.** People who agree to a small request are more likely to agree to a bigger one, because we like to be consistent. Saying yes once does not mean you have to keep saying yes ([BACS: Woche 31](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w31-de)).
- **Fear of missing out.** Fake investment platforms claim only a few people can join, so you fear missing a unique chance ([BACS: Woche 31](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w31-de)).

BACS sums it up: emotions are human, and that is exactly what scammers exploit ([BACS: Woche 32](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w32-de)).

## Shame helps the scammer

BACS advises not to act out of shame ([BACS: Woche 32](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w32-de)). Swiss Crime Prevention says it plainly: don’t blame yourself if you, or someone you know, fell for a romance scam. The police don’t punish human weakness, they hunt criminals. Your report can help protect other people ([SKP: Romance Scam](https://www.skppsc.ch/de/themen/internet/romance-scam/)).

Silence also hides how big the problem is. The US Federal Trade Commission notes that many frauds go unreported, so the reported amounts don’t show the true cost of fraud to older adults ([FTC: Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2026-older-adults-report-10192026.pdf)).

## What helps

- **Tell one person you trust.** BACS recommends talking to a trusted person if what happened weighs on you ([BACS: Romance Scam](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/romance-scam-de)).
- **Get help with the feelings too.** Swiss Crime Prevention suggests seeking psychological help if it is too much to carry ([SKP](https://www.skppsc.ch/de/themen/internet/romance-scam/)).
- **Get help with money problems.** BACS points to the debt advice service in your canton ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/romance-scam-de)).
- **Report it.** Phase 10 shows who to call, in Switzerland, the EU and the US.

:::exercise Words that help
Imagine a friend tells you: “I sent money to someone online. I feel so stupid.” Write down two sentences you could say back.
:::solution
Some ideas: “You’re not stupid. These people do this for a living.” “I’m glad you told me. Let’s look at what to do next, together.” “Lots of clever people have been tricked like this.” Avoid “How could you?” It adds shame and makes people stop talking.
:::end

:::helper
If someone shares that they were scammed, thank them for trusting you. Listen first. Then help with the next steps in Phase 10: the bank, the police report, and the passwords. Never take over their accounts without asking (lesson 11.01).
:::end
