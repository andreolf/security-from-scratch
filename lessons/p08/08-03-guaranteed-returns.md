:::story The seminar
Bruno is 66 and has just retired in Winterthur. At a free online seminar, a smart young man promises “12% a year, guaranteed, with no risk. Our places for this year are almost full.” Bruno’s savings account pays almost nothing, and the offer is tempting.

He asks for written details and says he will think about it. That evening he looks up the company on the FINMA website. It isn’t licensed, and it is on the warning list. Bruno deletes the emails.

*Bruno is made up.*
:::end

:::remember
**No one can guarantee investment returns. “Guaranteed”, “risk-free” and “act now” are warning signs, not selling points.**
:::end

## Risk and return always go together

The US Securities and Exchange Commission’s investor site is clear: every investment carries some degree of risk. If your money is perfectly safe, you will most likely get a low return. High returns mean high risks, possibly including losing everything ([Investor.gov: Avoid investment fraud](https://www.investor.gov/protect-your-investments/fraud/how-avoid-fraud/what-you-can-do-avoid-investment-fraud)). The FTC adds: only scammers will guarantee you’ll make big profits ([FTC: Investment scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/investment-scams)).

## The classic warning signs

Investor.gov lists the red flags of investment fraud ([Investor.gov: Red flags checklist](https://www.investor.gov/protect-your-investments/fraud/how-avoid-fraud/red-flags-investment-fraud-checklist)):

- offers that sound “too good to be true”
- “risk-free” opportunities, and promises of great wealth and guaranteed returns
- “everyone is buying it”
- pressure to invest right now
- sensational pitches with testimonials that may be fake
- pitches you didn’t ask for
- sellers who aren’t licensed

Investor.gov also warns of the “halo” effect: a con artist can seem likeable and trustworthy, and credibility can be faked ([Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/protect-your-investments/fraud/how-avoid-fraud/what-you-can-do-avoid-investment-fraud)). The Swiss cyber security office describes how fake platforms claim only a few people can join, to make you afraid of missing out ([BACS: Woche 31](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w31-de)).

## Check before you invest

- **In Switzerland:** check whether the firm is licensed, and look at FINMA’s warning list. FINMA explains that the firms on its list are not licensed and may be offering financial services without the required licence ([FINMA: Warning list](https://www.finma.ch/en/finma-public/warnungen/warnliste)).
- **In the US:** check the background and registration of anyone selling an investment on Investor.gov ([FTC: Investment scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/investment-scams)).
- **Everywhere:** search the company’s name plus “review”, “scam” or “complaint”, and talk to someone you trust before you decide, as the FTC advises ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/investment-scams)).

## Big losses, often for older people

Older adults in the US report losing far more money to investment scams than to any other type of fraud. Their reported losses to investment scams rose by 42% in 2025, often after contact on social media ([FTC: Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2026-older-adults-report-10192026.pdf)).

:::exercise Spot the flags
Read this advert and count the warning signs: “Join 10,000 happy investors! Our AI trading system earns 20% a month, guaranteed and risk-free. Only 15 places left today. Message our adviser on WhatsApp now!”
:::solution
At least five: “10,000 happy investors” (everyone is buying it); “20% a month” (too good to be true); “guaranteed and risk-free”; “only 15 places left today” (pressure); and “message on WhatsApp” (an unsolicited, unlicensed channel). The safe answer is to close it.
:::end

:::helper
If a relative is excited about an investment, ask three calm questions: “Is the firm licensed? Can we look it up together? What happens if you lose it all?” Offer to check the FINMA warning list together.
:::end
