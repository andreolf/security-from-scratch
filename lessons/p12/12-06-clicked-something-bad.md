:::story The free tokens
Mia is 64 and lives in Neuchâtel. A strange token appears in her wallet with a link: “Claim your reward.” She clicks, connects her wallet and approves. Then the site asks for her 12 words. That’s when she stops.

She calls her son. Together they make a brand-new wallet with new words, move her remaining crypto into it, and remove the site’s approval. They report it to the police. A week later, “a recovery agency” messages her. She ignores it.

*Mia is made up.*
:::end

:::remember
**Shared the phrase? It’s burned: move what’s left to a new wallet with a new phrase, now. Approved a bad site? Revoke it. Then report it.**
:::end

## Step 1: stop

Close the site. Don’t approve, sign or connect anything more. Take a breath; it is not your fault (lesson 08.02).

:::figure clicked-bad
Clicked something bad? Four steps.
:::end

## Step 2: if you shared the phrase, move everything

If you typed your secret phrase anywhere or told it to anyone, assume it is stolen. Trezor advises that if you suspect your backup has been compromised, you should assume it is and move your funds elsewhere immediately; acting promptly shortens the window in which funds can be drained ([Trezor](https://trezor.io/guides/backups-recovery/general-standards/move-crypto-to-a-wallet-with-a-new-wallet-backup)).

Create a **new wallet with a new phrase**, write the new words on paper, and send what is left to it. Changing the app’s password doesn’t help: the old words open the old wallet anywhere.

## Step 3: if you approved a site, revoke it

Some scam sites ask you to “approve” spending. ethereum.org explains that a site with unlimited approval can take those tokens even later, and recommends revoking access with a revoke tool: connect your wallet on the same network, choose the approval, and revoke it (this costs a small fee). Revoking should never cause a loss of funds ([ethereum.org: How to revoke token access](https://ethereum.org/en/guides/how-to-revoke-token-access/)). ethereum.org also advises not to allow unlimited spend limits in the first place ([ethereum.org: Security](https://ethereum.org/en/security/)).

If this is too technical, ask someone you trust to sit with you, and do it on a device you trust.

## Step 4: report it, and beware the second scam

Report it to the police and, in the US, to ic3.gov and ReportFraud.ftc.gov (lesson 10.03). The FTC also suggests reporting to the exchange you used ([FTC: Cryptocurrency and scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-scams)).

Then expect “recovery” offers. The FBI’s IC3 counted 10,516 recovery scam complaints in 2025, with $1.4 billion in losses ([FBI IC3 2025 report, p. 54](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). Nobody real charges a fee to get crypto back (lesson 10.05).

:::helper
If a relative calls in a panic about crypto, help with steps 1 and 2 first: stop, and move what’s left. Don’t answer “crypto recovery” offers or ads: those are the second scam (lesson 10.05).
:::end
