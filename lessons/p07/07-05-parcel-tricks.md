:::story CHF 1.95
Paul is 77 and lives in Chur. He is waiting for a parcel with new reading glasses. A text arrives: “Your parcel is held. Pay CHF 1.95 for redelivery,” with a link.

Paul taps the link. The page looks like the post office and asks for his card. He types it in, then the page says “Error, try another card.” That feels wrong. Paul calls his bank and blocks the card. The glasses arrive two days later, with no fee at all.

*Paul is made up. This trick is real.*
:::end

:::remember
**A parcel message with a link: don’t tap it. Open the delivery company’s own app, or type its address yourself.**
:::end

## The most common bait

Parcel messages are one of the most common kinds of [[term:phishing|phishing]]. The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) reports that phishing in the name of SwissPass and parcel services still made up around 40 percent of all phishing web addresses in the second half of 2025 ([BACS: Halbjahresbericht 2025/II](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/dam/de/sd-web/O6yLBijwgyl8/BACS-HJB-2025-2_DE.pdf)).

The messages say a parcel is waiting, a delivery failed, or a fee is unpaid. BACS explains that the link leads to a page that steals card details or signs you up for an expensive subscription, and that real delivery services and customs don’t collect fees this way ([BACS: Fake fees](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/en/ks22-1-en)). The US Federal Trade Commission describes the same messages in the name of USPS, FedEx or DHL ([FTC: Think that text is from USPS?](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/04/think-text-message-usps-it-could-be-scam)).

## The “second card” trick

The Zurich cantonal police describe a case like Paul’s. A message claimed an AliExpress parcel had not been collected. The fake page asked for a CHF 1.95 shipping fee and card details, then showed a fake error asking for the card again, or another card ([cybercrimepolice.ch](https://cybercrimepolice.ch/de/faelle/angeblich-nicht-abgeholtes-aliexpress-paket-fuehrt-zu-kreditkarten-phishing)). The police advise not to follow such links and, if you entered card details, to contact your bank and block the cards.

## How to tell a real message

Swiss Post lists the web addresses it uses: post.ch, poste.ch, posta.ch, swisspost.ch and swisspost.com. It says it will never ask for passwords, SMS codes or full card details by email, SMS or WhatsApp, and that real messages don’t contain threats or very short deadlines ([Swiss Post: Recognize phishing](https://site.post.ch/en/about-us/security/phishing-and-fraud/recognizing-phishing)).

In the US, the Postal Inspection Service says USPS only sends tracking texts if you asked for them with a tracking number, and those texts don’t contain a link ([USPIS: Package tracking text scams](https://www.uspis.gov/news/scam-article/smishing-package-tracking-text-scams)).

## Real customs fees exist

Here is what makes the trick work. Some parcels from abroad really do cost something. SECO explains that goods from abroad usually have to be declared and are generally subject to VAT and customs duty, and that carriers such as Swiss Post, DHL or UPS handle this and bill the recipient ([SECO: Einkaufen im Ausland](https://www.seco.admin.ch/de/einkaufen-im-ausland)). So don’t judge the fee. Judge the route: BACS advises comparing the consignment number with your own order and contacting the delivery service directly if in doubt ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/en/ks22-1-en)).

:::helper
Help a relative install the official app of the carrier they use most, from the App Store or Google Play (lesson 05.05). Show them where to type a tracking number. Then the rule is easy: “Parcel news? I look in the app.”
:::end
