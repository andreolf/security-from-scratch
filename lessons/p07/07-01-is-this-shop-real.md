:::story The garden bench
Lotte is 69 and lives in St. Gallen. A social media advert shows the teak garden bench she wants, at less than half price, from a shop called “Gartenwelt Schweiz”. The page has a Swiss flag, prices in francs and hundreds of five-star reviews.

Before ordering, she types the shop’s name and the word “scam” into a search engine. Two people write that they paid and got nothing. The website has no street address, only a contact form. Lotte closes the page and buys the bench from a garden centre she knows.

*Lotte is made up. Shops like this one are real.*
:::end

:::remember
**Before you pay a shop you don’t know: search its name plus “scam”, look for a real address, and don’t pay in advance by bank transfer.**
:::end

## A growing problem

Fake shops look like real ones. They take your money and send nothing, or something cheap and different. The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) received 824 reports of fraudulent web shops in the second half of 2025, 266 more than in the first half, with peaks from November to January, during Christmas shopping. It adds that many of these shops stress Swiss features, such as a flag or prices in francs, to build trust ([BACS: Halbjahresbericht 2025/II](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/dam/de/sd-web/O6yLBijwgyl8/BACS-HJB-2025-2_DE.pdf)).

A Swiss look proves nothing. The State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) points out that a web address ending in “.ch” does not mean the shop or company is Swiss, so you should check who the seller is, for example in the legal notice (the “Impressum”) ([SECO: Einkaufen im Ausland](https://www.seco.admin.ch/de/einkaufen-im-ausland)).

## Five checks, five minutes

1. **Ask the internet.** Search for the shop’s name plus the words “complaint” or “scam”, and see what other people say. This is the US Federal Trade Commission’s first tip ([FTC: Online Shopping](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).
2. **Look for a real company.** BACS advises checking the legal notice for a believable address, a working phone number and email, and a valid commercial register number ([BACS: Sicher online unterwegs](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/sicher-online-unterwegs)).
3. **Read reviews with care.** BACS warns that a large number of five-star reviews can itself be a warning sign, that bad reviews are the most useful ones, and that no reviews at all is suspicious too ([BACS: Woche 28](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w28-de)). The FTC adds that fake reviews exist, both good and bad ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).
4. **Check the address bar.** Look for small typos or extra words in the [[term:domain|web address]], and be careful with adverts in search results, because criminals buy those too ([BACS: Woche 28](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w28-de)).
5. **Look at how it wants to be paid.** The FTC says never to buy from sellers who insist on gift cards, wire transfers, a payment app or cryptocurrency, because those payments are hard to get back ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).

## How new is the website?

BACS notes that suspicious websites reported to it whose address was registered less than three months earlier are practically always fraudulent. For addresses ending in .ch, you can look up the registration date at nic.ch ([BACS: Woche 28](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w28-de)).

The [[term:padlock|padlock]] and “https” only mean the connection is encrypted. The FTC stresses that this does not mean the site is legitimate, because scammers can encrypt sites too ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).

:::helper
If a relative often shops online, sit with them once and do the five checks together on a shop they like. Save their favourite trustworthy shops as [[term:bookmark|bookmarks]], so they open the real site every time.
