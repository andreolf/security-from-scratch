:::story The parking QR code
Elsbeth is 70 and lives in Thun. At the car park, a sticker on the machine says “Pay with TWINT” with a QR code. She opens her phone camera to scan it, then remembers what her granddaughter told her: “TWINT codes only through the TWINT app.”

She opens TWINT and scans from there. The app shows the car park’s name and CHF 3.00. That matches the sign, so she pays.

*Elsbeth is made up.*
:::end

:::remember
**TWINT: scan QR codes only in the TWINT app, check the name and amount before you pay, and never share your PIN or a code.**
:::end

## What TWINT asks you to do

TWINT publishes its own safety rules ([TWINT: Sicher twinten](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)). In short:

- **Scan QR codes only in the TWINT app.** Not with the phone camera, which can open any website.
- **Check before you confirm.** Look at the name, the number and the amount every time.
- **Send money only to people you know.** Be especially careful on online marketplaces.
- **Don’t pay with codes someone sent you.** TWINT warns not to pay with 5-digit codes that someone else gave you ([TWINT](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)).
- **Real payment links** start with go.twint.ch, after the usual “https” beginning (lesson 02.02).
- **Keep your PIN secret.** Type it only in the TWINT app. TWINT, its partners and your bank never ask for your security code, and TWINT staff never ask for your passwords or access data.

## Why the camera rule matters

A [[term:qr-code|QR code]] is just a hidden web address. Anyone can print one and stick it on a machine, a poster or a letter. When you scan it with the TWINT app, the app shows you who you’re paying and how much, before you confirm. With the camera, you might land on a fake website that asks for your details instead (lesson 02.03).

## Money sent is hard to get back

The US Federal Trade Commission warns that scammers often ask you to pay with a payment app, a transfer or gift cards, because that makes it hard to get your money back ([FTC: Online Shopping](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping)).

On marketplaces, the Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security has seen criminals pretend to be buyers in a hurry and send a fake “Twint-Rechnung.zip” file that installs harmful software ([BACS: Woche 40](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w40-de)). Lesson 07.04 covers this trick.

If you think you were scammed, TWINT says to call your bank immediately. You can forward suspicious emails to abuse@twint.ch ([TWINT](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)).

:::exercise Three payments, three checks
Before each of these, which check matters most?

1. A friend asks for CHF 20 for the concert tickets she bought for you both.
2. A sticker QR code on a parking machine.
3. A stranger on a marketplace sends you a 5-digit code and asks you to “enter it in TWINT to receive the money”.
:::solution
1. Check it’s really your friend’s name and number in TWINT, and the amount is CHF 20.
2. Scan only from inside the TWINT app, and check the name and amount match the sign before you confirm.
3. Don’t. TWINT warns not to pay with codes someone sent you. A code from a stranger can send money from you, not to you.
:::end

:::helper
Set up TWINT together with a relative and walk through one small payment between you. Show them where the app shows the name and amount, and agree: “If anyone asks for a code or your PIN, call me first.”
:::end
