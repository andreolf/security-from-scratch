:::story Peter’s new friend
Peter is 73 and lives in Chur. His friend Ruth notices he has stopped coming to the walking group. He mentions a woman online who “really understands” him, and that he went to the bank twice for cash.

Ruth doesn’t say “That’s a scam.” She says: “You seem a bit worried. Want to have a coffee?” Over coffee, Peter shows her the messages. Ruth asks whether they have ever had a live video call. They haven’t. A week later Peter goes to the police, and Ruth goes with him.

*Peter and Ruth are made up.*
:::end

:::remember
**Notice the signs, ask gently, listen without blame, and offer to help with the next step.**
:::end

## Signs to watch for

:::figure scam-signs
Signs someone may be in a scam.
:::end

The US Justice Department lists warning signs of financial exploitation of older people. They include sudden changes in banking habits, unexplained withdrawals of large sums, money or possessions disappearing, bills left unpaid although there is enough money, and unexplained credit card charges ([DOJ: Red flags of elder abuse](https://www.justice.gov/elderjustice/red-flags-elder-abuse)). The same page lists emotional signs such as being upset, withdrawn, or sleeping or eating differently.

Scam-specific signs from earlier lessons:

- A new online “friend” or “partner” they have never met in person, who has an emergency (lesson 08.01). The FTC says: never send money or gifts to a sweetheart you haven’t met in person ([FTC: Romance scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams)).
- An investment with “guaranteed” returns, or an app that won’t let them withdraw (lessons 08.03 and 08.04).
- Buying gift cards or using a crypto ATM because someone asked (lesson 12.04).
- Secrecy: “I can’t tell you,” or “They said not to tell the family.” The FTC notes that fake-emergency scammers ask people to keep it secret ([FTC: Fake emergencies](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/scammers-use-fake-emergencies-steal-your-money)).

## How to talk about it

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) explains that romance scams build an emotional bond, and many people find it hard to get out alone ([BACS: Woche 32](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25w32-de)). So be patient.

- **Start with care:** “You seem worried. How are you?”
- **Ask, don’t accuse:** “Have you met in person? Can I see the messages?”
- **No blame:** it is not their fault (lesson 08.02).
- **Offer the next step:** call the bank together, go to the police together (Phase 10).

Swiss Crime Prevention advises: if you know a victim, encourage them to go to the police. If they don’t see the danger and risk serious money problems, you can contact the cantonal adult protection authority (KESB) ([SKP: Romance Scam](https://www.skppsc.ch/de/themen/internet/romance-scam/)).

:::helper
If they get angry or defend the scammer, don’t argue. Say: “I care about you. I’m here whenever you want to look at it together.” Keep in touch. Scammers want them isolated.
:::end
