:::story The key ring
Claudine is 70 and lives in Neuchâtel. Her daughter explains it with a key ring. “Every website has a ‘forgot your password?’ button. It sends a link to your email. So your email is like the ring that holds all your keys. If someone takes the ring, they can open every door.”

Claudine likes the picture. That evening, they give her email a new, unique password, and turn on two-step sign-in. “Now the ring is in a safe,” she says.
:::end

:::remember
**Your email is the master key to your other accounts. Protect it first, and protect it best.**
:::end

## Why email matters most

Almost every website lets you reset a forgotten password by email. That is convenient for you, and for a criminal who gets into your email. From your inbox, they can ask for new passwords for your bank, shops and social media.

:::figure master-key
Your email holds the keys to your other accounts.
:::end

This is what happened to “Emma” in CISA’s story: she couldn’t get into her email to receive the bank’s reset link, because the criminal already had it ([CISA: Use strong passwords](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords)). The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security advises: if your email password has leaked, change it and also reset the services that are linked to that email ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)).

## Three steps to protect your email

1. **A unique, strong password.** Long, random, and used nowhere else. Let your password manager create it (lesson 03.04).
2. **Two-step sign-in.** Also called two-factor authentication, 2FA or MFA. It asks for a second proof, such as a code or a tap on your phone, when someone signs in. CISA recommends turning it on for every account that offers it, including email ([CISA: Turn on MFA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/turn-mfa)). Phase 04 shows how, step by step.
3. **Up-to-date recovery details.** Your recovery phone number and email address help you get back in if you are locked out, and help alert you to suspicious activity. Google explains this in its account security advice ([Google Account Help](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/46526)).

:::try Check your email account’s security
These steps are for a Google account (Gmail), which works on any phone. If you use another email service, look in its settings for “Security”.

--- Google account
1. Sign in to your Google Account.
2. Go to **Manage your Google Account**, then **Security & sign-in**, or tap your profile picture and **Recommended actions** if you see it. This opens **Security Checkup**.
3. Follow the recommendations, especially **recovery phone** and **recovery email**.
4. If it suggests turning on **2-Step Verification**, you can do it now, or wait for Phase 04.

Checked against: [Google Account Help: Make your account more secure](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/46526).
:::end

## Watch out for fake “email security” messages

Criminals know that email is valuable. They send fake messages like “Your mailbox is full” or “Confirm your email password”. That is phishing. Use your own door: open your email settings yourself, never through a link.

:::exercise Draw your key ring
On a piece of paper, draw a circle and write “My email” in the middle. Around it, write every account that sends its password-reset emails to this address: bank, shops, health insurance, social media.
:::solution
Most people find five to fifteen accounts around their email. That picture shows why the email password must be unique and why two-step sign-in matters most here. If you have several email addresses, draw one ring for each, and start with the one that has the most keys.
:::end

:::helper
Check together which email address the bank and other important accounts use. Make sure the recovery phone number is your relative’s current number, not an old one. Write down, on paper kept at home, which email provider they use and where the recovery codes are, if any.
:::end
