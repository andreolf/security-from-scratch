:::story The old garden shop
Peter is 77. Years ago he made an account at an online garden shop. He used the password he uses for everything: his late dog’s name and his birth year.

The garden shop was hacked, and its customer list was stolen. Peter never heard about it. A few months later, someone signed in to his email with the same password. From there, they asked for a new password for his online banking. The reset link arrived in Peter’s email, which the criminal was now reading.

Peter’s bank stopped a large transfer just in time. “One old shop,” he says, “and all my doors were open.”
:::end

:::remember
**Use a different password for every important account. Then one leak opens only one door.**
:::end

## How one leak becomes many

Companies store your email and password when you create an account. Sometimes criminals steal that data. This is called a [[term:data-breach|data breach]], or a leak. It can happen to big and small companies, and it is not your fault.

Criminals then take the stolen email and password and try them on other websites: email, banks, shops. If you used the same password there, they get in.

:::figure one-key
One key for every door: one leak opens them all.
:::end

The US cyber security agency CISA tells a true-to-life story of “Emma”, who used the same password for her bank and her email. Her password was part of a company breach. Criminals got into both accounts, and it took her many hours to get them back ([CISA: Use strong passwords](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords)).

## What makes a strong password

CISA gives three tips. A strong password follows all three ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords)):

1. **Long:** at least 16 characters.
2. **Random:** not a name, a birthday or a word from your life. One way is a “passphrase” of four to seven unrelated words.
3. **Unique:** a different password for each account.

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security gives the same core advice: use a separate, strong password for each service ([BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/phishing-de)).

## “But I can’t remember all that!”

Nobody can. That is normal. CISA itself says it is impossible to remember a unique strong password for every account, and recommends a password manager instead ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords)). Lesson 03.03 explains what that is, and lesson 03.04 shows you how to set one up. It is easier than it sounds.

:::practice domino

:::helper
Don’t ask your relative to tell you their passwords. Instead, ask: “Do you use the same password in more than one place?” If the answer is yes, that is very common. Start with the two most important accounts: email and bank. Help make those two unique first.
:::end
