:::story “Just read me the code”
Margrit is 81 and lives in Lucerne. Her phone rings: “TWINT security here. Someone is trying to use your account. I’ve sent you a security code. Read it to me and I’ll block them.”

A code arrives a second later. Margrit nearly reads it out. Then she remembers her granddaughter’s rule: “A code is like a house key. You don’t hand it to a stranger at the door.” She says “No, thank you,” hangs up, and calls her bank on the number on her card. Her account is fine.

*Margrit is made up. Calls like this are real.*
:::end

:::remember
**Never read a code to anyone, never forward it, and never type it in because someone asked. Anyone who asks for your code is a scammer.**
:::end

## Why criminals want your code

A code by text message, or a pop-up in your banking app, is the second lock from lesson 04.01. If a criminal already has your password, the code is the only thing stopping them. So they call or message you and invent a reason to “need” it: blocking an intruder, refunding money, verifying your identity.

The code you get is for the sign-in that **they** started. When you read it out, you let them in.

:::figure code-rule
Only you should ever see a sign-in code.
:::end

## What the real companies say

TWINT writes that TWINT, its partners and your bank will never ask you for your security code, and that you should keep your TWINT PIN to yourself and type it only in the TWINT app ([TWINT: Sicher twinten](https://www.twint.ch/privatkunden/support/sicherheit/sicher-twinten/)).

Google says you should not share your backup codes with anyone, and that Google never asks for a backup code other than when you sign in ([Google Account Help: Sign in with backup codes](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/1187538?co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid&hl=en)).

The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security warns about “real-time phishing”, where criminals use fake pages and calls to collect your login details and codes while they sign in at the same moment ([BACS half-year report 2025/2](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/hjb-25-2-de)).

## The same rule for pop-ups

Some banks and apps don’t send a code but ask you to tap “Approve” or “Confirm”. The rule is the same: only approve something you started yourself, just now. If a pop-up appears out of nowhere, or because a caller told you it would, tap “Reject” or do nothing, and call your bank on the number on your card.

:::practice call-drill scenario=code

:::helper
Put a small note next to the phone: “I never read codes to anyone.” Agree on a family rule: if anyone, even family, asks for a code, the answer is no. Show your relative what a real code message looks like, so they recognise it.
:::end
