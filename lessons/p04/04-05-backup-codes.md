:::story The phone in the lake
Bruno is 74 and lives in Thun. On a boat trip his phone slips out of his jacket and into the lake. The next day, on a new phone, his email asks for a code from… the old phone.

Luckily his daughter had printed a sheet of backup codes the year before. It is in the folder with his passport. Bruno types one in, and he is back in his email in a minute. “A spare key under the doormat,” he says, “but a doormat only I know about.”

*Bruno is made up.*

:::remember
**Keep a spare key: printed backup codes or a trusted recovery contact. Store codes with your passport, and never give them to anyone who asks.**
:::end

## Why you need a spare key

Two-step sign-in sends the second step to your phone. If the phone is lost, stolen or broken, you need another way to prove it’s you. Google suggests backup codes for exactly this: when you lose your phone, change your number or can’t get codes ([Google Account Help: Sign in with backup codes](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/1187538?co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid&hl=en)).

## Backup codes (Google and many others)

Backup codes are a short list of one-time codes. Google explains that each 8-digit code works only once, that you can get a new set of 10 whenever you want, and that the old set then stops working. It suggests printing them and keeping them where you keep your passport or other important documents ([Google Account Help](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/1187538?co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid&hl=en)).

Many other services, such as banks or social media, offer similar “recovery codes”. The idea is the same.

## A recovery contact (Apple)

For an Apple Account, you can choose a **recovery contact**: a person you trust who can get a recovery code for you if you are locked out. Apple says the recovery contact has no access to your account, only the ability to give you a code ([Apple Support: Set up a recovery contact](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102641)).

## The golden rule still applies

A backup code is just as powerful as a sign-in code. Google says it never asks for a backup code other than when you sign in, and that you should not share your codes with anyone ([Google Account Help](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/1187538?co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid&hl=en)). If someone calls or writes asking for one, it’s a scam.

:::try Make your spare key
--- iPhone
Add a recovery contact for your Apple Account:
1. Open **Settings**, then tap **your name**.
2. Tap **Sign-In & Security**, then **Recovery Contacts**.
3. Tap **Add Recovery Contact** and follow the steps. Choose someone you trust; they accept the invitation in Messages.

Checked against: [Apple Support: Set up a recovery contact](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102641).

--- Android
Get backup codes for your Google Account:
1. Open the **Settings** app, tap **Google**, then **Manage your Google Account**.
2. At the top, tap **Security & sign-in**.
3. Under “How you sign in to Google”, tap **2-Step Verification**. You may need to sign in.
4. Under **Backup codes**, tap **Continue**, then **Get backup codes**. Print them, or write them down.

Checked against: [Google Account Help: Sign in with backup codes](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/1187538?co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid&hl=en).
:::end

:::exercise Where does the spare key live?
Pick a place at home for your backup codes. Which of these is best: (a) a photo in your phone’s gallery, (b) the folder with your passport, (c) an email to yourself?
:::solution
(b), the folder with your passport. If the phone is lost, a photo in its gallery is lost too, and an email may be locked behind the very account you are trying to open. Paper at home works when nothing else does. Write on it which account the codes are for, and when you made them.
:::end

:::helper
Offer to be a recovery contact, if your relative uses an Apple device and you do too. Print the backup codes together and put them in the important-papers folder. Remind them: you will never ask for one of those codes, and nobody else should either.
:::end
